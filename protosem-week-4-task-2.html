3D Printing a Sensor Enclosure

A snap-fit enclosure for a 5-channel Line Array Sensor: from a STEP file and a vernier calliper, to Bambu Studio, to a 13.08 g print that fit first time.

01Overview

For ProtoSem, I chose a 5-channel Line Array Sensor and made an enclosure for it, designed in Fusion 360 and printed in 26 minutes 30 seconds.

A line array sensor reads the surface beneath it, so the enclosure had to protect the board without affecting its sensing. I expected the sensor’s CAD model to be enough for an exact fit, and I was honestly unsure how to make the top and bottom close with a snap fit.

02Design Requirements

Hold it securely

A mounting extrusion for the sensor’s mount holes to sit on keeps the board in place.

Keep openings clear

Openings for the pins, sensor and LEDs, so sensing is not blocked.

Open and close easily

A snap fit between the top and bottom parts.

Don’t affect sensing

The sensor reads the surface beneath it; a blocked or misplaced opening would affect it.

03Design in Fusion 360

I imported the existing 3D CAD model of my sensor, a STEP file, into the Fusion design workspace. A model alone does not guarantee a perfect fit, so at the same time I measured the real sensor with a vernier calliper.

04Slice & Print

I imported both files into a Bambu Studio project, placed them properly on the base, sliced everything and generated the G-code, then transferred it to the printer through a USB drive. Step through it below.

The two-part enclosure

The top and bottom shells modelled in Fusion 360 around the sensor, with openings for the pins, sensor and LEDs.

Sliced in Bambu Studio

Both shells sliced in Bambu Studio: PLA, 0.2 mm layers, 15% infill. The G-code went to the printer on a USB drive.

On the Bambu Lab printer

I selected my model on the printer and started the print. It took 26 minutes 30 seconds.

The finished enclosure

The finished enclosure with the line array sensor board. It weighed just 13.08 g, and nothing needed a second try.

06What I Learned

01The calliper made it fit

A CAD model of the sensor is only a starting point. Checking it against the real sensor is what makes the dimensions, clearances and mounting work together.

02Design to print, end to end

Exporting the top and bottom as high-refinement STL (binary) files, slicing them in Bambu Studio and printing over USB.

03Design doesn’t end when the model looks right

The sensor’s job is to read the surface beneath it, so an opening that was blocked or in the wrong place would have affected its sensing.

04Next

I am keen to take this into a robot with sensors, where the mechanical design and the electronics have to work as one.
